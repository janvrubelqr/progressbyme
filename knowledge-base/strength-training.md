# Strength training

## Key facts

**Why it matters with age**
- Muscle loss starts around the mid-30s: ~1–2%/year, strength ~1.5%/year.
- After 60–70, muscle loss accelerates to ~3%/year.
- Muscle *power* (strength × speed) declines even faster than raw strength — up to 3.5%/year vs 1.5%/year — which is why balance/fall-prevention training benefits from power moves (fast concentric reps, plyometrics), not just slow heavy lifting.
- Without training, adults lose roughly 4–6 lb of muscle per decade.

**Dosing (what actually works, per Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans / ACSM)**
- Strength training: full-body routine, 20–30 min, 2–3x/week, ≥48 hours rest between sessions for the same muscle group.
- Power training: 2–3x/week alongside strength training (can be the same sessions, different tempo).
- Reps: 8–12 for strength goals; 6–10 (faster tempo) for power goals.
- Tempo for strength: 3 seconds lifting, 1 second hold, 3 seconds lowering. For power: lift as fast as possible with good form, still 1 second hold / 3 seconds lowering.
- Rest between sets: 1–3 minutes for best strength gains (30 seconds if prioritizing cardio/calorie burn instead, at some cost to strength gains).
- Fastest strength gains happen in the first 4–8 weeks, then progress slows — this is normal, not a plateau to worry about.

**Safety fundamentals**
- Breathe out on exertion, in on release — never hold your breath while straining (can spike blood pressure dangerously; relevant given hypertension overlap).
- Keep a slight bend in knees/elbows — never lock joints under load.
- Normal soreness starts 12–48 hours after a workout and fades; pain *during* a workout, or soreness lasting >1–2 weeks, warrants stopping and checking with a doctor.
- People with heart disease, uncontrolled high blood pressure, joint/bone disease, or diabetes should get medical clearance before starting, especially before more intense routines (kettlebell, medicine ball, plyometrics).
- People with poorly controlled high blood pressure should generally avoid straining isometric holds (the opposite advice from the blood-pressure report's "isometric helps BP over time" — the difference is: a single max-effort isometric strain spikes pressure acutely; *regular, moderate* isometric training lowers resting BP over weeks).

**Documented health benefits (useful for in-app copy)**
- Diabetes: strength training improves insulin sensitivity; combined with cardio, cuts type 2 diabetes risk ~59% (men) vs ~52% cardio alone / ~34% strength alone.
- Bone density: weight-bearing/resistance exercise slows age-related bone loss and can build a small amount of new bone — especially valuable post-menopause, when bone loss can hit 20% over 5–7 years.
- Osteoarthritis: stronger muscles (especially quadriceps) reduce joint load and cartilage wear; strength training eases pain and improves function even after a joint replacement.
- Depression: high-intensity strength training reduced depressive symptoms more than low-intensity, in adults 60+.
- Metabolism: strength training raises resting metabolic rate up to 15% and produces a bigger "afterburn" (extra calories burned post-workout) than equivalent cardio.

**Exercise fibers, in plain terms**
- Slow-twitch fibers: endurance, low-intensity, aerobic — walking, cycling.
- Fast-twitch fibers: short bursts of power — sprinting, jumping, catching something falling.
- Strength training mainly builds slow-twitch capacity; power training (faster reps, plyometrics) builds fast-twitch capacity. Daily life uses both.

**Program structure that's proven to work**
- Warm up 5–10 min (dynamic movement, not static stretching) before lifting.
- Do strength/power work while warmed up but before you're fatigued.
- Stretch at the end (muscles are most pliable then), holding each stretch 10–30 seconds, repeated to a total of ~60 seconds.
- A well-rounded program also needs ~150 min/week moderate aerobic activity (separate from strength training) — the two are complementary, not substitutes for each other.
